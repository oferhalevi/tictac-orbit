How this works

A 3,000-year-old pencil-and-paper game, rebuilt as a glowing machine in space. Every part of it — the board, the opponent, the sound — is code you could open and read.

The board

Nothing here is a video. three.js draws a real 3D scene onto your screen with WebGL — your graphics card renders nine glass tiles, a starfield, and a bloom pass that makes bright things genuinely glow. Drag to orbit: the camera is a satellite circling the board in real time.

When you click, a ray of mathematics is cast from your cursor through the scene to find which tile you meant. That's raycasting — the same trick shooters use to detect bullets hitting walls.

What "vs AI" means

The O you face isn't faking it. It runs minimax: before every move it silently plays out every possible continuation of the game — every reply you could make, every counter to that reply, all the way to the end — then picks the move where the worst thing that can happen to it is still the best available.

The practical upshot: it never loses. Draw it, or lose to it — there is no third outcome if you slip. Beat it once and you'd have proven the mathematicians wrong.

Tic-tac-toe is tiny enough to solve exhaustively — the AI searches it in well under a second. In 2 players mode, the machine stays out of the way entirely.

The sound

No audio files. Every click and chime is synthesized live with the Web Audio API: X lands as a low triangle wave, O as a higher sine, and a win triggers a three-note major chord — 523, 659 and 784 hertz, staggered 120 ms apart. Mathematics, played as music.

The craft

The animation is spring physics, not canned loops — tiles scale in with overshoot, marks drop onto the board, the winning beam shoots through the line before the gold pulse begins. If your system asks for reduced motion, all of it settles instantly instead. Your score persists in this browser between visits.
